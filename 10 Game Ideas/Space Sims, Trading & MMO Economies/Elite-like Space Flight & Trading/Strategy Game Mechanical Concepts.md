Strategy requires a dynamic environment. The player needs to be given choices that matter both where and when they are made. Complex, gradual and slow state changes are probably the best kinds in this environment to allow the human mind to grasp and predict the changes. Patterns will form in the battle environment and an experienced player will take advantage of these.

In many strategy games, ammunition and hit points determine a ships effectiveness. These tend to allow 100% efficiency until the point that one of them runs out, changing effectiveness to 0%.

Heat and Energy management might be formed into a suitable mechanic to allow this environment to exist.

Reactors and other components can handle extremely high levels of use, but they cannot handle extremely high rates of change. A reactor might be able to output 1.5GW of power but perhaps a civilian version will only be able to increase its output by 0.01GW per second, up to the maximum, while a military variant could change 10 times faster.

Damage to systems can occur if you change their output or parameters too fast. The player must have systems “preset” so that they are effective at the right moment. This creates a sweet spot in the combat where the ship has its maximum “opportunity conversion rate” or simply it is the most opportune time for it to perform XYZ action.

Systems may also have a “redline” or “redzone”. Operating the system above this line/zone will cause heavy but gradual damage. Keen players may utilize this to “damage load” their opportunities, increasing effectiveness now, and paying for it later.

Cratering armor is a good system, however it is still very arbitrary. When the armor reaches zero, damage starts to occur to the internal systems. Soft limits or in this case, barriers feel much better to me. The player must decide if the armor is too low to risk continuing combat. In a hit point system, the player can easily decide if the enemy can inflict enough damage in one salvo to destroy his ship. A soft cap not only hides some intel from the player about incoming damage, but also promotes more careful gameplay. This gives a greater advantage to the older, more experienced players.

Positional Strategy is popular with space games. Fire arcs, and limited movement/turning in ships creates a game where both players are trying to sniff each others tails and end up in a repetitive space ballet. An ideal situation is to be in the right place at the right time, but often it feel like ANY time is the best time to be in the right place.

If positional strategy is combined with variable opportunity conversion rates, things might be different. Suddenly the right place is only good at the right time, and in fact may be the wrong place to be at any given moment.

Instead of a system simply being “charged” or “not charged” perhaps another mechanic could be used. That of investment. Players must invest energy over time to be sure that the systems they need are available at the right time.

In WWII fighter pilots gained “energy” over the course of their battle. This was gained through airspeed or altitude and the total of while you had over a whole engagement was limited by your fuel reserves. The total that you could have stored at any time was limited by the strength and design of your airframe. This energy could be “used up” to create opportunities for attack and defence. Carefully aiming at your opponent would use up a lot of energy with small movements, diving away from an enemy attack would also. The attackers would often be in heavy and slow bombers, while the defenders were nimble fighters launched from the defending airfields. Why were the defenders at such disadvantage? Because the attackers had already spent a lot of time in the air, their energy reserves were much higher. The defenders were only joining the battle and had to build up their reserves from zilch. Much of this energy was simply spend gaining the altitude to fight their enemy, and could not be spend on attack or defence.

This Opportunity energy could be applied to a space strategy game to create battles with differing start, middle and end gameplay. The attacking fleet is entering the battle area with reactors battle ready, heat sinks empty and weapons/shields pre-charged. The defenders, while having the numerical advantage, have been holding with their systems discharged to conserve lifespan, their ships geared toward detection rather than direct conflict.

Now we have a battle where the attacker must seize his advantage to do as much damage as possible, retreating when it looks like the tide has turned.

The defender must decide how much strain to place on his hardware by bringing reactors to combat readiness too fast, where to shunt energy so that it is available in the correct systems to take advantage of opportunities that have not occurred yet.
